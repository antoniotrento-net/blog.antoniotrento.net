A pretty site isn't a product. It's the most common trap in digital: the agency delivers a splendid showcase, you pay ads to bring people there, and on the second click — when the user tries to *do* something — the thing breaks, stalls, or simply does nothing. Because under the varnish there is no engine. This guide collects the articles on how you move from the showcase site to the **digital product that converts**: a serious frontend, tied to a real backend, that makes whoever arrives do the thing they arrived for — and do it all the way.

## The principle: the product is the face, but behind it you need the engine

The thread that ties these articles is the distinction between **showcase** and **product**. The showcase shows you and that's it: the visitor reads, looks, and leaves. The product *makes you do* something — book, buy, manage, produce a result — and to do that it needs two things the showcase doesn't have: a **usable face** (the frontend, with its states, errors, empties, permissions) and a **real engine** behind it (the backend, the data, the logic). When the face is missing, you have an engine the client doesn't know how to use (the "desert"). When the engine is missing, you have a face that collapses on the first real click. A product is *both*, stitched together.

The other thread is that the value, in a product, sits in the invisible 80%: not the ideal case where everything goes well, but the crooked states — what the user sees while it loads, when they make a mistake, when they have no data, when they don't have permissions. That's where a product closes or loses people at the last metre.

## The piece that decides everything: the states, not the ideal case

Under every product that converts sits the discipline of **states**. The pretty case — data present, everything works, user with the right permissions — is 20% of the work. The other 80% is what happens when things go crooked: loading, the error that has to say *what to do* instead of "something went wrong", the empty state that guides the new user, the permissions that show each person only what they can do. A product made by whoever cuts these corners works in the demo and collapses on the first real day. That's why serious frontend isn't "a bit of CSS": it's where the experience is born or dies.

And the most expensive errors live in the **stitching** between face and engine: the backend that refuses because of a rule of its own (slot taken, expired code) and the frontend that doesn't know how to explain why, so it shows a wall instead of an action. This only gets solved with a single direction that holds both sides together — the same pair of hands I describe throughout the [guide to buying custom software]({{ '/en/pillar/buying-custom-software/' | relative_url }}).
